Meet-GSplat · August 2026

Picking the boring trainer for a car-meet 3D scanning booth

The instinct is to reach for the research pipeline. At a car meet, that instinct is wrong.

Meet-GSplat turns a phone's worth of photos of a car into a shareable 3D scan. The booth version of the workflow is: capture at the meet, train on a home RTX 4090, clean the result, and hand the owner a QR code that opens a branded 3D viewer. Free scans expire and carry a watermark; paid ones don't, and can be embedded elsewhere. That's the product. The interesting part was everything upstream of "which trainer do I use."

The bottleneck isn't the GPU

Gaussian splatting has an obvious "proper" answer: an owned pipeline built on gsplat and Nerfstudio's Splatfacto, trained from COLMAP reconstructions, Apache-licensed and fully automatable. That's the pipeline I'd reach for by default, and it's still on the roadmap as a parallel track for batch automation later.

It's the wrong first choice for a booth. At a meet, the constraint isn't training throughput — it's an operator standing next to a car, capture quality that has to be judged in seconds, and a line of people waiting. A CLI research pipeline with fragile setup steps turns every one of those seconds into risk. So the primary trainer is PostShot, a commercial GUI tool with a live preview, specifically because it minimizes booth complexity, not because it's more "engineered." The fewer moving parts between a bad photo set and a visible warning, the fewer ruined scans.

Licenses that would have quietly wrecked the model

The other thing a research pipeline doesn't warn you about is licensing, and this is where it gets genuinely dangerous for a paid product. The original INRIA Gaussian splatting trainer — the one most tutorials point to — is non-commercial only. Ship that under a paid tier and the business model has a landmine in it from day one. OpenSplat, an open alternative, is AGPL, which drags copyleft obligations into a closed product if you're not careful. PostShot itself has a free tier that's non-commercial and limited on export — fine for testing, not for a meet where money changes hands.

None of this shows up in a feature comparison. It only shows up if you read license text before you build on top of something, which is a boring habit that's saved me from a much worse conversation later.

Baking the business model into the server

The free/paid split isn't a marketing page bolted on afterward — it's enforced server-side, per model: a 72-hour TTL and watermark on free scans, no embed route, an 80 MB cap; no TTL, no watermark, an embeddable viewer, and a 200 MB cap on hosted ones. An operator can upgrade a free scan to hosted with one action and no re-upload, because the annoying failure mode is a customer who already has a link and now has to start over.

Operating alone, under pressure, on a home GPU

The whole thing runs on a home RTX 4090, tunneled out under a named domain so QR codes resolve for someone on cellular data, not localhost. A health endpoint reports worker heartbeat, queue depth, disk space, and GPU state — the stuff you actually want to check between customers. And there's a deliberate "meet mode" switch that lets the operator (usually me) pause the upload queue mid-event if something's going wrong, rather than discovering a backlog after the fact. Payment collection is still manual — Venmo at the booth — which is the correct amount of engineering for a system that's proving out a workflow, not the amount of engineering that looks impressive in a portfolio.

The best tool for a one-person booth under time pressure is whichever one fails the least visibly.
